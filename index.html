Tahoe Timber Planner

A Forest Service silviculturist estimated in 2023 that there are now about 50 to 75 trees per acre, compared with likely about 20 before Comstock-era logging. Other estimates differ. Trees felled in the Tahoe region are typically hauled 70 to over 100 miles away, the Tahoe Daily Tribune reported in October 2026. Local crews make nail-laminated timber (NLT) panels from that wood. None are for sale yet, and Tahoe Wood is working to build that pipeline and workforce. Sketch a backyard home or ADU from those panels. The planner checks what a Tahoe lot allows, sizes the roof for snow, and lists every panel with a price guide and the estimated carbon in the wood. Everything snaps to a 4-ft plywood module, so the same grid carries over to local CLT.

    Ridge beam and posts
    Logs and NLT beams use local wood but span less than glulam, so heavy snow means more posts. The planner adds posts on a 2-ft half-panel grid until the beam works.
    Ridge support
    A center bearing wall carries the roof along the beam line with no posts, and a short header spans its doorway. It splits the home lengthwise, for example bedroom and bath on one side and living on the other. Tied rafters skip the ridge beam entirely. The two roof slopes lean on each other at the peak, and steel ties across the room at wall-top height keep the walls from spreading. They work on gable roofs only.

      Utilities
      All-electric with heat pumps, so there's no gas line or meter. Septic generally isn't an option in the Tahoe Basin. California law sends basin sewage to sewer systems that carry it out of the watershed, with narrow exceptions, and both states ordered sewage exported in the 1960s. Plan on connecting to sewer. Ask your water provider whether your lot has public water or a private well. The planner sites the home near the house to keep the utility trench short. California law requires any ADU connection charge to be proportionate to the unit's burden, based on its square footage or plumbing fixtures.

        Ways to lower the cost
        • Stay at 750 ft² or under. California bars local impact fees on ADUs with 750 ft² or less of interior living space.
        • Keep it a simple rectangle on the 4-ft grid. Fewer panel sizes should mean less waste and simpler installation.
        • Leave the NLT ceiling exposed. The structure becomes the finished wood ceiling, so there's no drywall or paint overhead.
        • Use a pre-approved standard plan once one is ready. None exists for NLT yet, and one is in the works. Repeating a plan should cut architect, engineering and plan-check time.
        • Tie into the main house's sewer, water and electric, and site the ADU close to the house. The trench stays short and there are no new meters.
        • Go all-electric with heat pumps. There's no gas line, meter or trench.
        • If local rules allow two homes, build both at once. They share site work, utility runs and mobilization.
        • Take the weathertight shell and finish the inside yourself, or with a local finish carpenter.
        • Deed-restrict it as workforce housing. That can open fee waivers, bonus units and housing grants where they're offered.

          Price and engineering assumptions

          NLT panel pricing follows Tahoe Wood's draft NLT financials: rough-sawn local lumber at about $1 per board foot (a verbal local quote from about May 2026), plus nails, labor, transport and overhead, comes to about $11–12/ft² of 6-in panel. The $15/ft² default leaves a small producer a margin. Work through the numbers yourself in the NLT producer calculator. Deeper roof panels cost more in proportion to their extra lumber. "Rest of build" covers walls, windows, roofing, insulation, mechanical, electrical, plumbing and finishes. The range shown is −15% to +20% around the midpoint.

          Panel depths are a theoretical screening calculation on the plywood-size module, to show the method. They are not an engineering design. For NLT detailing, see Think Wood's NLT Design and Construction Guide. The calculation assumes:

          • Hem-Fir No. 2 lumber with NDS size factors and a 1.15 snow load-duration factor.
          • A 0.8 reduction on strength and stiffness for butt joints.
          • ASCE 7-22 flat-roof snow pf = 0.7·Ce·Ct·pg with no slope reduction. Ce = 1.0, or 1.2 when the lot is sheltered among conifers. Ct = 1.2, the upper end of ASCE 7-22 Table 7.3-3 for a heated, insulated, unventilated roof. Loads are applied at 0.7 for allowable-stress design, because ASCE 7-22 ground snow values use a load factor of 1.0.
          • Gable roofs at 7:12 or flatter are also checked for ASCE 7-22 §7.6.1 unbalanced snow: the full ground snow load on the leeward side, with the windward side unloaded. This is applied to every gable layout as a conservative simplification.
          • Snow deflection increased 1.5× for creep, limited to span/240.
          • A floor load of 40 psf, limited to span/480, with spans capped at 16 ft.

          When the roof needs a ridge beam or beam lines, the planner sizes a 24F-V4 glulam (Fb 2,400 psi, E 1.8 million psi) for bending, shear and span/240. Round logs use placeholder values for visually graded local pine or fir (Fb 1,000 psi, E 1.1 million psi) until logs are graded. NLT beams are 3 to 8 plies of 2x12 Hem-Fir No. 2 nailed together. CLT beams are a preview. They use a 5-ply, 6⅞-in panel on edge with generic PRG 320 E1 values (Fb 1,950 psi with a 0.85 reduction, E 1.7 million psi) until a local CLT mill's panels are certified. Tied rafters treat the gable as a three-hinged frame. A36 steel rods at wall-top height (allowable 0.33 × 58 ksi on the gross area) take the outward push, H = w·W²/(8·rise), under balanced snow. It adds evenly spaced interior posts on a 2-ft half-panel grid, each with its own pier or footing, when a single span won't work. The walls are load-bearing. They carry the roof panels and the beam ends, but wall capacity, headers over openings, earthquake and wind bracing, and connections aren't checked. A licensed engineer will replace all of this with stamped span tables.
